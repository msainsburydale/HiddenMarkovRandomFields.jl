Performance ​

The timings are wall times of one MCMC iteration in milliseconds (ms), excluding initialisation, for a hidden Potts model with   labels, Gaussian emissions and  , close to the critical value. Gibbs only is a chequerboard Gibbs sweep (sw_every = 0), and Gibbs + SW adds a Swendsen–Wang sweep (sw_every = 1, the default). They were measured on an AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 5995WX CPU, on 1 thread and on 32 threads, and on an NVIDIA RTX A4500 GPU, with Julia 1.12.6 and CUDA.jl 6.5.1.

Summary ​

  • GPU. A GPU is the fastest for all but the smallest workloads, often by far: up to 17× faster than 32 threads. Only for 30 chains of 32 × 32 pixels with Swendsen–Wang are 32 threads faster, by about 10%.

  • Threads. With many chains, 32 threads are up to 28× faster than 1 thread. A single chain uses several threads from about 64 × 64 pixels, and is then up to 16× faster on 32 threads.

  • Swendsen–Wang makes an iteration 5–7× as expensive as a Gibbs sweep alone on 1 thread, and up to about 20× on 32 threads and 30× on a GPU, where Gibbs sweeps parallelise better. It pays off near and above the critical value, where Gibbs sampling alone mixes slowly (see Burn-in and Swendsen–Wang updates).

  • Unconditional simulation costs about as much as conditional simulation with Gibbs sweeps only, and up to about 40% less with Swendsen–Wang, since there are no likelihood weights to add up over the clusters.

  • Parameters per chain cost nothing extra on the CPU, and up to about 5% on a GPU.

Parameters per chain ​

Each chain can have its own β and emission distributions (see Unconditional simulation), at no extra cost on the CPU and for up to about 5% more time per iteration on a GPU. One call of simulatehiddenpotts that simulates a batch of training data, 1024 fields of 64 × 64 pixels with 200 iterations, each field with its own β (uniform on (0, 1.5)) and emission distributions, takes 0.62 seconds on 32 threads and 0.18 seconds on a GPU, including initialisation and drawing the data.

Using a GPU ​

  • Keep the results on the GPU. simulatehiddenpotts returns arrays on the GPU, and copying them to the CPU, e.g. with Array(Y), can take longer than several iterations. Use them there, e.g. in a neural network, and continue the chains with PottsChains, whose drawZ! draws the data on the GPU.

  • Memory. Per pixel and chain, the labels take 1 byte, and the Swendsen–Wang sweeps another   bytes. The output of simulatehiddenpotts takes 8 bytes per pixel, chain and saved iteration for Y, plus the size of the data type for Z.

Reproducing the results ​

benchmark/table.jl measures the times, writes them to benchmark/results.csv, from which the figure is drawn, and prints the table. From the top level of the repository:

bash
# On the CPU (the first command is needed only once)
julia --project=benchmark -e 'using Pkg; Pkg.develop(path = "."); Pkg.instantiate()'
julia --project=benchmark --threads=auto benchmark/table.jl
# With an NVIDIA GPU, in the environment benchmark/gpu, which has CUDA.jl
julia --project=benchmark/gpu -e 'using Pkg; Pkg.instantiate()'
julia --project=benchmark/gpu --threads=auto benchmark/table.jl
